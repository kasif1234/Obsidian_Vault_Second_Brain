**Literature Review**

The article _Journalism, Nationalism, and Multiperspectivity: A Critical Discourse Analysis of Media Coverage of Kulbhushan Jadhav Case in Pakistani and Indian Media_ by Sameen Shah provides an important academic foundation for understanding how media framing and bias operate in the context of Pakistani-Indian conflicts. The main purpose of the study is to examine how mainstream print media in both countries reported the Kulbhushan Jadhav case and how such reporting was shaped by nationalism and selective framing. The author focuses on two Pakistani newspapers, _Dawn_ and _The News_, and two Indian newspapers, _The Indian Express_ and _Hindustan Times_, using critical discourse analysis to compare their headlines from 18 May to 20 May 2017.

A central concept in the article is **multiperspectivity**, which refers to understanding an event through multiple viewpoints rather than a single dominant narrative. Shah explains that this concept is especially useful when studying media because news coverage often reflects political, cultural, and national interests rather than objective truth. The article connects multiperspectivity with critical discourse analysis, showing that language in media is not neutral but instead carries power, ideology, and influence. In this way, the study argues that journalism does not simply inform audiences, but also helps shape how national issues are interpreted by the public.

The article also places the Jadhav case within the wider historical tension between Pakistan and India since 1947. According to the paper, the arrest of Kulbhushan Jadhav in Balochistan, his accusation as an Indian spy, and the later involvement of the International Court of Justice created an ideal case for studying nationalistic reporting. Because the incident was politically sensitive and emotionally charged, newspapers in both countries used language carefully to support their own national narratives. This makes the article highly relevant to our research topic, since it shows how conflict reporting can become an extension of rivalry itself.

Another important contribution of the article is its close analysis of headlines. Rather than studying full articles, Shah chooses headlines because they are designed to attract attention quickly and strongly influence first impressions. The paper shows that even a single word can reveal bias. For example, _The News_ refers to Jadhav as a “spy,” while Indian newspapers avoid that label. Likewise, Indian newspapers describe the ICJ ruling as a “victory” and use emotionally charged expressions that present Pakistan in a suspicious and negative light. These examples support the idea that framing happens not only through facts included or excluded, but also through word choice, tone, and emphasis.

Overall, this article is valuable because it combines theory and real media examples to demonstrate that media coverage in conflict situations is rarely neutral. It supports our research by showing that media in both Pakistan and India often reproduce nationalism, mistrust, and political agendas through their reporting. At the same time, it highlights the importance of reading news critically and considering how different perspectives are constructed.

**Discussion of Findings**

The findings of Shah’s study clearly show that media framing in Pakistani-Indian conflicts is deeply shaped by nationalism. Although both Pakistani and Indian newspapers reported on the same Jadhav case, they presented it in very different ways. Pakistani newspapers often framed Jadhav as an “Indian spy,” which immediately positioned him as guilty and dangerous. On the Indian side, newspapers avoided such labeling and instead framed the ICJ decision as a diplomatic success for India. This shows that bias is not always obvious false reporting. Rather, it appears through selective language, emotional wording, and the angle through which the event is presented.

One key finding is that Indian newspapers appeared more openly celebratory after the ICJ ruling, using phrases such as “Round One to India” and suggesting that Pakistan might not obey the court’s order. This reveals not only bias but also an attempt to build a narrative of moral and diplomatic superiority. Pakistani newspapers, on the other hand, seemed less consistent. According to the author, their tone shifted from assertive to defensive, especially when the ruling did not go in Pakistan’s favor. This inconsistency is also meaningful because it reflects the political pressure on national media during sensitive international disputes.

These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of our topic by proving that media coverage of conflict is not just about delivering information. It is also about shaping public emotion, reinforcing national identity, and influencing how citizens view the opposing country. In this sense, the media becomes an active participant in the conflict narrative rather than a neutral observer. The article therefore helps explain why audiences in Pakistan and India often develop sharply different understandings of the same event.

For our broader research on framing and bias in media coverage of Pakistani-Indian conflicts, this study is especially useful because it gives a concrete example of how discourse operates during a diplomatic crisis. It suggests that media consumers should not accept headlines at face value, especially during conflicts involving nationalism and historical hostility. Instead, they should compare sources, question loaded terms, and remain aware of the political interests behind media narratives. This is one of the most important lessons we can draw from the article.
